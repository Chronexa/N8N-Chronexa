# Phase 0 — Framing the Research

Done 2026-07-12, before any web research. The goal here wasn't to answer anything — it was to make sure we were asking the right questions before spending research effort. Full reasoning delivered in chat; this is the record of the decisions and the "why" behind them, so future us doesn't have to re-derive them.

## The problem with the original brief
The original brief listed five labels — wealth management, investment advisory, investment research, portfolio management, and adjacent financial services — as if they were one industry. They're not. An RIA (an independent advice firm charging clients a yearly fee, usually around 1% of the money it manages) makes money completely differently from a research shop (sells subscriptions to reports) or a fund's portfolio management team (earns fees on a pooled fund, not individual client relationships). Researching all of them at once would have produced shallow, generic findings. We picked one to go deep on first.

The brief also listed 11 job roles (Founder, COO, Operations, Compliance, Investment Research, Portfolio Management, Wealth Advisors, Client Servicing, Finance, Technology, Back Office) — that's an enterprise org chart. A firm with 10-75 staff has 5-6 people wearing multiple hats, not 11 separate departments. Researching 11 roles as if they were 11 separate people would misrepresent how these firms actually work.

## Decisions locked with Ankit
1. **Geography: US.** Matches Chronexa's existing customer base (CPAs, law firms) and the advisory-fee business model actually exists at scale in the US. (Abhishek's SEBI — India's securities regulator — RIA license is still useful for content credibility, just not as the target market.)
2. **Anchor business type: independent RIA**, roughly $100M–$3B in client assets, 10-75 staff. Other business types (research boutiques, fund operations, family offices) deferred to later research passes.
3. **Chronexa's typical deal size confirmed by Ankit: $10,000-$75,000 per engagement.** This sets the floor for which firms are worth targeting — a firm needs enough revenue that spending this doesn't require a board vote. Working assumption: roughly $150M+ in client assets managed.

## Assumptions we're carrying, and why they might be wrong
- **"Manual work = opportunity."** Not always true here — some manual steps exist because a regulator requires a human to check something (e.g., a compliance officer reviewing a trade or an ad before it goes out). We're tagging every workflow we find as "manual by neglect" (nobody bothered to automate it) vs "manual by regulation" (a human is legally required to be in the loop) — the second kind is still sellable, just needs a "human approves, AI drafts" framing, the same pattern we already use in Chronexa's own Outbound Engine (the n8n workflow that finds and emails cold leads — every AI-drafted email sits in a queue for human approval before it sends).
- **"Busywork saved = money saved."** In a business that earns a % of assets managed, the real ceiling on revenue is how many clients each advisor can personally handle — not how many hours the back office works. So automation that lets an advisor take on more clients, or reduces the odds of a compliance mistake, is worth more than automation that just saves someone an afternoon of data entry.
- **"The competitor is Accenture."** Almost certainly wrong. A mid-market firm's real alternative to hiring Chronexa is hiring another ops person, waiting for their existing software to add an AI feature, hiring a small RIA-specific ops consultant, or doing nothing. Big consulting firms aren't in the actual decision.

## Blind spots flagged before researching (so we'd know to look for them)
- **Custodian friction.** For a US RIA, client money is actually held by a separate company (a "custodian" — Schwab, Fidelity, Pershing are the big three). A ton of daily pain lives in the paperwork exchanged with the custodian, especially when opening new accounts — forms get rejected as "NIGO" (Not In Good Order) and bounce back and forth. This is invisible if you only read software vendor marketing pages.
- **The tool market is already crowded.** There's a well-known reference map (the Kitces AdvisorTech landscape, published by industry analyst Michael Kitces) tracking hundreds of vendors selling into this space already. If we pitch something a vendor already ships as a feature, we look naive. We need a real map of who already sells what.
- **Vendor-biased sources.** Most public writing about these firms comes from software companies selling into them. We're weighting practitioner voices — actual operators, industry benchmarking studies, forums — over vendor blog posts.
- **Timing.** These firms have a yearly rhythm (an annual regulatory filing update happens around Q1, billing often happens quarterly, tax season creates a rush) and trigger events (a firm getting bought by a private equity investor, or an advisor leaving a big brokerage to start their own independent firm — called a "breakaway") that likely open buying windows.

## Refined research objective
Understand one specific, well-defined business — an independent US RIA managing $100M-$3B, with 10-75 staff — well enough to map its full client relationship lifecycle (get a client → open their account → build them a plan → invest their money → check in → report to them → bill them → stay compliant), tag every step by how much pain it causes and whether a regulator requires it to stay manual, and match that against what software already exists to fix it. Two extra things we're explicitly building: a glossary of the real words these operators use (so our content sounds like an insider, not an AI vendor), and a map of who already sells automation into this space.
