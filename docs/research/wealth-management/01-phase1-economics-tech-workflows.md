# Phase 1 — Economics, Tech Stack & Core Workflows

Research run 2026-07-12 via automated multi-source research (23 sources fetched, 109 factual claims pulled out, 25 of the most important claims independently fact-checked by three separate reviewers each — 23 held up, 2 were wrong and thrown out). Full source list and methodology notes at the bottom.

**One-paragraph summary:** The target firm (independent US RIA, $100M-$3B in client assets, 10-75 staff) is a healthy, growing business — but it's bottlenecked on people-hours, not on demand. The best-run firms in the data prove automation is the lever: they handle almost double the clients per staff member as everyone else, just by having their systems actually wired together. Almost every firm already owns the right software; most just never finished setting it up, so the office still runs on email and spreadsheets sitting next to expensive tools nobody configured properly. That gap — bought, not built out — is Chronexa's opening.

## 1. How the money works (unit economics)

*Source: Charles Schwab's 2024/2025 RIA Benchmarking Study (self-reported data from 1,300+ firms who bank with Schwab) and InvestmentNews' 2025 Advisor Benchmarking Study (77 firms). Both are the industry's most-cited annual studies — treat as high confidence, but note they only cover firms that already work with Schwab or chose to respond to a survey, which likely skews toward larger, more organized firms.*

- A typical advisor-level professional at a mid-size firm ($250M+) manages **$108M in assets and serves 61 clients**.
- They spend about **29 hours a year per client on direct service** (meetings, calls, questions) and **15 hours a year per client on operations and admin** — the paperwork-and-process side, which is where automation lives.
- Median profit margin hit a record **27.8%** in 2024. Revenue per professional crossed **$1 million** for the first time.
- The whole industry grew fast in 2024: **client assets up 16.6%, revenue up 17.6%**, but the number of actual clients only grew **4.8%**. Translation: firms are getting richer mostly because markets went up and existing clients added more money, not because they're taking on a lot of new people.

**Why this matters for us:** the growth isn't the problem. The problem is that 15 hours-per-client-per-year of operations work doesn't shrink just because a firm gets bigger — it multiplies. Real example: if Rahul-the-advisor (not a real client, illustrative) has 60 clients, that's 900 hours a year of pure admin work spread across him and his support staff — more than half a person's whole working year, before he does a single hour of actual advice.

### The proof automation works: top performers vs. everyone else
Firms Schwab classifies as "Top Performing" (the top 20%, chosen for better growth and profitability, and who — Schwab notes — "more often use digital tools and workflows"):
- Spend **25% less time per client on operations** (11 hours vs. 15) and **more** time on actual client service.
- InvestmentNews' top performers serve **nearly double the clients per staff member**, while running at **25.7% overhead** vs. **45%** for everyone else.

*Caveat: this is correlation, not proof of cause — top firms might just be better-run in general, not better-run because of automation specifically. But it's the industry's own framing of the prize, which makes it very usable in our content ("the top 20% of firms already do this — here's how").*

## 2. The tech stack is bought, not built out — this is the opening

*Source: Schwab 2024 study, verified word-for-word against the original report.*

Almost every mid-size-plus firm already owns the core software:
- Portfolio management software: **99%** of firms have it
- CRM (Customer Relationship Management — basically the firm's central address book + task list + "who do I need to call today" system): **97%**
- Financial planning software: **93%**
- Document storage, trading/rebalancing tools, client web portals: 80-89%

But owning the tool isn't the same as using it properly:
- Only **38%** of firms under $250M in assets have **standardized workflows** in their CRM for more than half their repeat tasks (versus **63%** for the top-performing firms).
- Only **35%** of those smaller firms have **3 or more systems actually talking to each other** (versus **51%** for top performers).

**In plain terms:** most firms this size bought a CRM, and it turned into what one industry consultant called (see below) "a glorified Rolodex" — just a contact list, not the operating hub it's supposed to be. The tools to fix this exist and are already paid for. Nobody configured them. **That configuration-and-connection work is exactly what Chronexa does.**

## 3. The mess is neglect, not regulation

*Source: Kate Guillen, an ex-RIA operations manager turned consultant, interviewed on the Kitces podcast (Kitces is the most-respected independent research and commentary site in this industry — think of it as the trade publication operators actually trust, not a vendor blog).*

She described the typical firm asking her for help: **"they have an Outlook calendar, they're using Asana for tasks, they keep an Excel spreadsheet of their pipeline... their CRM is being used as basically a glorified Rolodex... they're shouting down the hall"** to hand off work between people.

No SEC rule forces any of this. It's just habit and neglect — nobody had the time or knowledge to wire the tools together properly. This is good news for us: **we're not fighting compliance law here, we're fixing sloppy IT.** (Phase 2 is checking the flip side — which steps actually ARE required to stay manual by law.)

### The real client-lifecycle checklist (this is our automation-opportunity list)
The CRM is supposed to be the hub for four things: calendar, tasks, sales pipeline, and contacts — with billing, trading, and reporting living in separate connected systems. The actual repeatable events firms process, over and over, for every client:

**New client:** onboarding (the very first thing an operations consultant builds automation for — usually in month two of an engagement)
**Ongoing life events:** address changes, account transfers, check deposits, adding money, withdrawing money, closing accounts, ending the relationship, required annual withdrawals for retirement accounts (RMDs — Required Minimum Distributions), death of a client, divorce, converting a retirement account type (Roth conversion), retirement transitions, updating who inherits the account (beneficiary changes).

Every one of these is a real, named, recurring workflow — and each is a candidate blog topic ("what actually happens when a client dies, operationally" is a real, searchable, insider-grade topic these firms live with constantly).

## 4. How big does a firm need to be before it hires dedicated ops help?

*Source: Charesse Spiller, an RIA operations consultant, on Kitces.*

- A firm typically doesn't hire a **full-time, dedicated** Director of Operations until it crosses **roughly $2 million in revenue**. Below that, whoever does ops is doing it as a second job alongside another title.
- A **solo advisor working alone** tends to hit a breaking point — sticky notes and Excel start dropping balls — somewhere around **30-40 clients** (roughly $220K-$320K in personal revenue). *(Important: this 30-40 number is the ceiling for someone working completely alone — it does NOT apply to our actual target firms, where supported advisors handle 60+ clients because they have staff and systems behind them.)*

**Why this matters for our sales pitch:** a firm below $2M in revenue almost certainly doesn't have a dedicated operations person to fight with internally — the founder or a generalist is doing it badly on the side, which likely makes them an *easier*, not harder, sale.

## 5. The gap in the existing "automation help" market

*Source: Charesse Spiller (Level Best, an RIA ops consultancy) on Kitces, 2023.*

This is the single most useful finding for how Chronexa should position itself. Spiller distinguishes:
- **"Integrations"** — two systems that pass data to each other, but a human still has to click a button to make it happen.
- **"Automation"** — the task actually goes away; nothing waits on a human anymore.

Then the admission: **her own consultancy doesn't build the actual automation.** She refers all of that work out to a specialist partner (a company called Sphynx Automation, confirmed as a real, active business doing RIA-focused Zapier work, project sizes roughly $500-$15,000).

**Translation for us:** the leading operations *strategy* consultant in this space openly told her audience she can map out what needs automating but can't build it — and hands that exact work to someone else. That's not a competitor moat, that's a referral pipeline with a sign on it saying "automation builder needed here."

### What firms currently pay for this kind of help
*Confidence: medium — this pricing data is from April 2023, so treat as a rough historical anchor, not current market rate (Phase 2 is checking for 2025-2026 pricing).* Level Best's pricing at the time: $5,000+ for a strategy audit, $2,000-$3,000/month for 3-4 months of workflow mapping, then $5,000+/month for 6-12 months of actual implementation and training — a **$30,000-$60,000 total engagement**. Spiller specifically noted smaller firms told her they **couldn't afford** that. That's a real, named price umbrella Chronexa can price under, and a real, named underserved tier (firms too small for a $30-60K consultant, too complex for a $500 Zapier freelancer) that fits our ICP.

## 6. Who's actually installed — the vendor map

*Source: the 2026 T3/Inside Information Software Survey — the industry's biggest and most-respected annual technology survey (2,906 advisor responses, run by two well-known industry analysts, Bob Veres and Joel Bruckenstein). Numbers below verified digit-for-digit against the original report.*

**CRM:** Redtail leads with **42.5%** market share among respondents but gets only a **7.35/10** satisfaction score (mediocre) — meaning the market leader is also the one people are least happy with. Wealthbox (17.5% share, 8.12 rating) and Advyzon (10.2% share, 8.35 rating) are smaller but better-liked.

**Financial planning software:** eMoney has just overtaken the long-time leader MoneyGuidePro (35.6% vs. 24.2% share) — a real, recent shift in the market. RightCapital, the #3 player, actually has the *highest* satisfaction score of the three (8.40/10) despite the smallest share.

**Strategic read:** the highest satisfaction scores of any tool in the entire CRM category (9.0+) belong to add-on products that sit *on top of* Salesforce and add deep workflow customization. **Firms are already paying extra, on top of their base software, for someone to build them better workflows.** That's proof of willingness to pay for exactly what we do.

## 7. AI is already moving fast in this market

*Source: Schwab's 2025 study and the 2026 T3 survey.*

- **68% of RIA firms already use AI in some way.** The most common uses: admin support (43%), writing marketing content (38%), client correspondence (31%).
- The single fastest-adopted new product category the T3 survey has ever tracked: **AI meeting-notetaking tools.** From effectively nonexistent a year ago to **42.86% of firms using one** — led by two products, Jump and Zocks, that basically didn't exist for this market until recently.
- **But adoption is wide and shallow.** A separate Schwab study found only about **1 in 10 firms have actually built AI into their strategy** — most usage is scattered, one-off tool adoption, not systematic. Where AI actually gets deeply woven into how a firm works, it's rare.

**Why this matters:** don't pitch "AI notetaking" — that door is already closed, two well-funded startups own it. But it proves this buyer moves fast and adopts AI readily *when the tool clearly fits a painful workflow.* The opening is everywhere AI hasn't been woven into the actual operating workflow yet — which per the 1-in-10 stat above is nearly everywhere except notetaking.

## 8. A real example, in full: VisionPoint Advisory Group

*Source: Erika Wood, Director of Wealth Management at VisionPoint, on Kitces (December 2024). This is the single richest case study in the research, and worth reading in full because it's about as close to "walking into a real firm" as public research gets.*

**The firm, sized for context:** Dallas-based, hybrid RIA, 35 staff, about 780 client households, just over $600 million in the assets they manage directly for private clients (roughly $3 billion total across everything they oversee), $8.8 million revenue in 2023, projected over $11 million for 2024. That's roughly $250,000 of revenue per staff member — squarely inside our target ICP.

**What they run:** HubSpot as their CRM (unusual — most firms use industry-specific tools like Redtail; VisionPoint chose HubSpot deliberately for its flexibility after centralizing their service team) plus eMoney for financial planning.

**The concrete automation they already built, and why:** their support ticketing system **will not let an advisor submit a client service request unless all the required information is filled in** — no incomplete tickets allowed. That single guardrail is expected to cut down on exactly the kind of "sent it back, missing information" paperwork bounce-backs (NIGO — Not In Good Order, industry slang for any form rejected as incomplete) that plague this industry. *(Caveat: this is the firm's own expectation of what the guardrail will do, not an independently measured result.)*

**The bottleneck they admitted out loud, on record — this is the single best quote in the whole research pass:**
VisionPoint centralized meeting prep (building the agenda, pre-meeting worksheets, reviewing the client's financial plan for anything that needs flagging) into one small internal team — but they **ration it**. Only clients above roughly **$750,000** in assets get that white-glove prep. Everyone below that has to prepare their own meetings. In Wood's own words: **"you're going to have to do the prep yourself because we only have so many people."**

That is a real operator at a real firm in our exact target size, on the record, saying: *we know this work should happen for every client, we don't have enough hands to do it, so we ration it by client size instead.* That is a textbook AI-automation opportunity, described by the buyer herself, unprompted.

**Also useful — how they set prices**, since it shows the "cost to serve" thinking that AI automation could measurably move: they build fees bottom-up from a spreadsheet of *"each team member's hourly rate × estimated time per client,"* plus a profit margin. Tiers run from $2,500/year (basic planning) up to $12,000-$25,000/year for business-owner exit planning work. If Chronexa can shrink the "estimated time per client" side of that spreadsheet, we're directly moving their pricing math and their margin — a very concrete, provable ROI (Return On Investment) story for a sales pitch.

## Glossary (plain-English definitions, first use)
- **RIA (Registered Investment Adviser):** an independent firm that gives investment advice and charges clients a fee, registered with the SEC (or a state regulator, if small enough).
- **AUM (Assets Under Management):** the total dollar value of client money a firm manages. The main number used to size a firm.
- **CRM (Customer Relationship Management):** the software meant to be a firm's central hub for contacts, calendar, tasks, and client pipeline.
- **Custodian:** the separate company that actually holds client money and securities (Schwab, Fidelity, Pershing are the big three). The RIA gives advice; the custodian holds the assets.
- **NIGO (Not In Good Order):** industry slang for any form or paperwork that gets bounced back because it was filled out incorrectly or incompletely.
- **RMD (Required Minimum Distribution):** a legally mandated yearly withdrawal from certain retirement accounts once the owner reaches a certain age.
- **Breakaway advisor:** an advisor who leaves a large brokerage (a "wirehouse," like Merrill Lynch or Morgan Stanley) to start or join an independent RIA.
