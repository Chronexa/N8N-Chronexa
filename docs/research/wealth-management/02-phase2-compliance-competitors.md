# Phase 2 — Compliance, AI Adoption Gaps & Competitors

Research run 2026-07-12, closing gaps from Phase 1 plus four new areas Ankit asked for (client reporting, investment research workflow, AI adoption gaps, competitor/gap analysis). 26 sources fetched, 100 candidate claims pulled, the 25 most important independently fact-checked by three reviewers each.

**Be upfront about this phase's quality, because it matters for what we do next:** this pass was noticeably weaker than Phase 1. Only **10 of 25** checked claims survived (versus 23 of 25 last time) — **15 got thrown out**, mostly stats from blog posts that couldn't be traced back to anything real when checked. Two whole topics — how client reports actually get made, and how investment research/portfolio decisions actually get made — came back with **zero** surviving evidence. That's a real result, not a failure to search hard enough: it tells us this information doesn't live in public articles. **The honest conclusion is that closing those two topics requires talking to an actual operator, not more web searching** — see "What this means for next steps" at the bottom.

## 1. What's genuinely required by law vs. what's just habit — now answered (for SEC; IRS still open)

This was Phase 1's biggest open question. Good news: for SEC rules, we now have a real, sourced answer.

**Two things really are legally mandated, no way around them:**
- **Rule 204-2 (the "books and records" rule):** Whenever a firm posts a testimonial or endorsement (think: a client review, an influencer partnership, a "success story" on the website), the firm is legally required to keep documented proof it had a "reasonable basis" to believe that testimonial was truthful and compliant *before* publishing it. This isn't optional — it's a specific, named legal requirement (17 CFR 275.204-2(a)(15)(ii), verified against the actual regulation text). The rule also requires keeping roughly 10 categories of records generally: financial ledgers, client communications, every version of required disclosure forms, advertising records including social media, and more.
- **Rule 206(4)-7 (the "annual compliance review"):** every RIA is legally required to review its own compliance policies at least once a year and write down that the review happened. Not a best practice — a hard requirement.

**The twist, and it's an important one for how we pitch this:** SEC examiners checking on firms found that even though these reviews are legally required, **many firms just... don't do them properly.** Some never updated their policies to cover new rules. Others updated the paperwork but never actually followed it in practice. So even the *legally mandated* stuff often falls into neglect anyway. **This is actually good news for Chronexa** — it means we're not just automating optional admin work, we're helping firms actually satisfy a legal obligation they're currently failing to meet. That's a much stronger pitch than "save time."

**One important nuance to carry into content and sales conversations:** the law requires the *review itself* to happen and be documented — it does not say a human has to do 100% of the legwork by hand. The part that must stay human is the final sign-off judgment ("yes, this was adequate"). Everything that leads up to that judgment — gathering the records, checking them against a list, flagging gaps — is fair game for automation.

**Still open — the IRS side.** We specifically tried to find how manual things like required retirement withdrawals, tax-loss harvesting (selling a losing investment to offset taxes, one of the most common and repetitive tasks in this industry), 1099 tax forms, and cost-basis reporting really are. **Every single claim we found on this got thrown out on fact-check** — not because the topic isn't real, but because the sources (mostly vendor blog posts) couldn't back up their numbers. This needs a different kind of source next time — the actual IRS text, custodian technical documentation, or talking to a CPA who works with these firms (we already have a foothold here — see the note on CPA overlap in the framing doc).

## 2. Where AI is trusted, and where it's actively rejected — this is now much clearer

Three separate, credible 2025-2026 industry surveys agree on the same pattern, which makes this one of the most solid findings in the whole project:

- Advisors are fine with AI in the back office — but rate "letting AI fully replace our core software" as the **single least popular idea of anything tested** (essentially rejected).
- Advisors are lukewarm at best on AI touching **compliance work specifically** — even though real AI tools built just for this exist and get genuinely high user ratings (two named products, Greenboard and Hadrius, both rated highly by the people using them), almost nobody has adopted them yet (both under 1.5% of firms). **The tools exist and work. The trust doesn't exist yet.** That's a meaningful gap for content: "the tools are ready, here's why nobody's using them" is a real, defensible angle.
- **Client-facing service is the one area advisors flatly don't want AI touching at all** — confirmed again in this pass (see Phase 1, section 7). Worth repeating because it's now backed by two separate surveys: don't pitch anything that puts AI in front of the end client directly.
- Most advisors, when asked what they actually want from AI, said they want it to **"expedite," not replace,** human work. That's a precise, quotable phrase we should probably reuse in our own positioning — it's literally what the buyer said they want.

## 3. One real buying-trigger example (the rest came back empty)

We were specifically hunting for what makes a firm decide *now* is the time to buy automation help — a firm getting bought by private equity, an advisor leaving a big brokerage to strike out alone (a "breakaway"), that kind of thing. Almost everything we found on this got thrown out on fact-check (two often-cited stats — "54% of firms expect more M&A" and "two-thirds are at a succession breaking point" — didn't hold up when checked).

**One real, named example did survive:** Axos Securities (a custodian — the company that holds client money) told a trade publication they now use AI to automatically read client documents (names, addresses, tax ID numbers) and pre-fill account transfer paperwork specifically when a breakaway advisor is switching custodians — cutting what used to take months down to a single weekend in one case.

**Treat this as one data point, not a trend** — we could not confirm this is happening industry-wide, just that one custodian is doing it for one specific moment (the custodian switch). Still useful: it's proof a real financial institution already sees "AI reads the document and fills the form" as valuable enough to build, in exactly the kind of onboarding paperwork pain we flagged as the top suspect back in Phase 0.

## 4. Competitors — mostly still an open question

We were trying to map out who else is already selling AI/automation help into this market, with pricing, so we know what we're up against. **This came back thin.** We confirmed two named AI-native *compliance* tools (Greenboard and Hadrius, mentioned above) exist and are well-liked but barely adopted. We did not get verified, current information on ops-consulting competitors' pricing beyond the 2023 Level Best numbers already in Phase 1. **This is still an open gap — flagged below for next steps.**

## What didn't survive fact-checking (for the record — don't reuse these)
Fifteen claims were checked and thrown out this round, including: an AI-adoption "under 25%" statistic, a claim that investment research software is "near-universally adopted" (the opposite direction of what we needed to know), a claim that custodians broadly (not just Axos) already sell AI-assisted onboarding, a specific SEC-examiner AI-documentation checklist, two different M&A/succession statistics, and two wash-sale/tax-loss-harvesting claims. All of these came from blog-quality sources that couldn't be traced back to anything solid — a reminder that this industry has a lot of vendor content restating numbers nobody can verify.

## What this means for next steps
Two research passes have now shown a pattern: **web research is running out of road on the harder questions.** The topics that keep coming back empty (client reporting production, investment research workflow, IRS-side compliance detail, who actually approves a purchase) are exactly the kind of thing that lives in someone's head, not in a blog post — how a specific team actually spends its Tuesday. The next step that will move this forward fastest isn't a third web-research pass on the same four questions — it's **talking to one real operator** (an ops person or COO at a firm in our target size range) for even 30-45 minutes. If anyone in the InvestMates or Abhishek's RIA network fits, that single conversation would likely answer more of the open list above than another round of searching. Worth deciding whether to pursue that or keep pushing web research with better-targeted sources (IRS.gov directly, custodian technical docs, CPA practitioners for the tax side).
